## 1. Catalogues and APIs: what public catalogues/data exist for pulsars, FRBs, RRATs and long-period transients, and how to access them programmatically

### Takeaway
Pulsars have one authoritative, scriptable catalogue (ATNF psrcat v2.8.1, 18 June 2026, 4,393 pulsars, via the `psrqpy` Python package or a tarball), FRBs have one dominant survey catalogue (CHIME/FRB Catalog 2, January 2026, 4,539 bursts from 3,641 sources incl. 83 repeaters) plus the Transient Name Server as the naming authority, RRATs have the revived RRATalog (335 sources, CSV on GitHub, April 2026), and long-period transients (about 12–15 objects) have no machine-readable catalogue at all, only review-paper tables. Timing-array data (NANOGrav 15-yr, EPTA DR2, PPTA DR3, MeerKAT PTA) are small, open Zenodo/portal downloads, whereas raw search-mode archives (Parkes: >4.5 PB; FAST releases) are open after 12–18 month embargoes but are petabyte-scale and need accounts.

### Cited Findings

#### Pulsar catalogues
- **[V]** ATNF Pulsar Catalogue: the official history page lists **version 2.8.1, released 2026-06-18, with 4,393 pulsars**; the only listed change is a bug fix to derived distance parameters. No later version could be checked because the host was unreachable from this session — [ATNF catalogue history](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/research/pulsar/psrcat/catalogueHistory.html)
- **[V]** The catalogue grows quickly: a December 2025 SKA population paper cites v2.6.0 with "more than 3800 objects", and an undated ATNF news item quotes 3,748 pulsars and "over 200 parameters" — [arXiv:2512.16156](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16156v1); [ATNF psrcat page](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/research/pulsar/psrcat/)
- **[V]** Reference paper to cite: Manchester, Hobbs, Teoh & Hobbs, "The ATNF Pulsar Catalogue" — [arXiv:astro-ph/0412641](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0412641)
- **[V]** Programmatic route 1 (bulk file): the whole database is a tarball at `https://www.atnf.csiro.au/research/pulsar/psrcat/downloads/psrcat_pkg.tar.gz`; archived versions follow the pattern `downloads/psrcat_pkg.v{VERSION}.tar.gz` (these are the URLs hard-coded in psrqpy 1.3.3's `config.py`) — [psrqpy on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/psrqpy/)
- **[V]** Programmatic route 2 (`psrqpy`): v1.3.3 uploaded 2026-08-26, MIT licence, Python ≥3.8, 62 releases since Nov 2017, also on conda-forge; "unofficial … not endorsed by or affiliated with the ATNF". It downloads and caches the full tarball (cache dir via `appdirs`, `checkupdate=True` re-downloads when the hash changes, `version="x.y.z"` pins a catalogue version) and exposes it as a pandas DataFrame / astropy Table. Example from its README: `psrqpy.QueryATNF(params=['Jname','f0'], condition='f0 > 100 && f0 < 200', assoc='GC')`; `q.catalogue` returns the full table; `query.ppdot()` draws a P–Pdot diagram — [psrqpy on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/psrqpy/); source [github.com/mattpitkin/psrqpy](https://github.com/mattpitkin/psrqpy)
- **[V]** Fields useful for a signal-search tool (from psrqpy's parameter table): period/frequency and derivatives (P0, P1, F0–F5), DM and derivatives, RM, pulse widths W50/W10, scattering time TAU_SC, flux densities (S40…S1400, S2000), glitch parameters (NGLT, GLEP, GLF0…), binary parameters, distances from three electron-density models (YMW16, NE2001 and the new `DIST_DM_NE2025`), and a TYPE code with values AXP, BINARY, HE, NRAD, RADIO, **RRAT**, XINS — [psrqpy on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/psrqpy/)
- **[V]** psrqpy also wraps three companion tables: the Jodrell Bank glitch catalogue (`https://www.jb.man.ac.uk/pulsar/glitches/gTable.html`), Paulo Freire's globular-cluster pulsar list (`https://www3.mpifr-bonn.mpg.de/staff/pfreire/GCpsr.txt`) and a Galactic millisecond-pulsar list — [psrqpy on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/psrqpy/)
- **[V]** A NASA HEASARC mirror of the catalogue exists (listed on data.gov as "ATNF Pulsar Catalog"); the `astroquery.heasarc` module (astroquery 0.4.11, BSD, 2025-09-20) queries HEASARC at `https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/xamin/vo` — [data.gov entry](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/atnf-pulsar-catalog); [astroquery on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/astroquery/)
- **[V]** EPN Database of Pulsar Profiles: hosted at Jodrell Bank, `https://psrweb.jb.man.ac.uk/epndb/` (older address `www.epta.eu.org/epndb`). A 2023 paper counts **2,458 integrated profiles for 840 pulsars from 77 references**. Profiles can be viewed in-browser or downloaded as PSRFITS, plain text or the original format; the site needs JavaScript and provides data "as-is" with no quality guarantee — [EPN database](https://psrweb.jb.man.ac.uk/epndb/); [arXiv:2311.09201](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.09201)
- **[V]** `pulsar-spectra` (v2.1.0, 2025-09-03, MIT): a pip-installable compilation of published pulsar flux-density measurements with spectral-model fitting (Swainston et al. 2022, PASA 39, e056) — [pulsar-spectra on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pulsar-spectra/)

#### Pulsar timing array (PTA) data releases

Contents: TOAs (`.tim`), timing models (`.par`), template profiles, clock files and noise-model MCMC chains; ASCII, for Tempo2 or PINT. Earlier versions are ~606–607 MB — [Zenodo 16051178](https://zenodo.org/records/16051178); [Zenodo 8423265](https://zenodo.org/records/8423265)

- **[V]** EPTA DR2 (25 millisecond pulsars, individual spans ~14–25 yr; the GW analysis quotes 24.7 yr): Zenodo record 8300645 holds `EPTA-DR2.zip` (~39.3 MB); also cited as DOI 10.5281/zenodo.8164424 and cloneable from the IN2P3 GitLab (`gitlab.in2p3.fr`, `epta/epta-dr2`) — [Zenodo 8300645](https://zenodo.org/records/8300645); [EPTA DR2 page](https://epta.pages.in2p3.fr/epta-dr2/); [arXiv:2306.16224](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.16224)

- **[V]** IPTA DR3 (combining EPTA, NANOGrav, PPTA, InPTA, MeerKAT PTA and CPTA; >100 pulsars) was still described as a future release in a February 2026 preprint; no public release was found as of this research — [arXiv:2602.14014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.14014v1)
- **[V]** MeerKAT PTA: the 4.5-year release covers **83 millisecond pulsars and 245,907 TOAs** (median uncertainty 3.1 μs), in Tempo2-compatible format with IPTA metadata (MNRAS 536, 1467, 2025). The first MPTA release was hosted at the Australian Gravitational Wave Data Centre — [arXiv:2412.01148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.01148); [arXiv:2212.04648](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.04648)
- **[V]** MeerTime's portal `pulsars.org.au` has a command-line/GraphQL client, `psrdb` (v3.0.6, 2024-05-13, MIT), whose default endpoint is `https://pulsars.org.au/api/` — [psrdb on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/psrdb/)

#### Parkes/Murriyang, ASKAP and the CSIRO portals
- **[V]** Parkes Observatory Pulsar Data Archive (paper posted Nov 2025, published in PASA): **over 4.5 PB (4,624 TB), nearly 2 million files, from ~450 pulsar projects**, in PSRFITS format, downloadable from the CSIRO Data Access Portal (DAP). Data are embargoed for **18 months** (extendable or removable; PULSE@Parkes is released immediately). The DAP is Virtual-Observatory compatible (queryable with TOPCAT) — [arXiv:2511.22702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.22702); [PASA version](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/publications-of-the-astronomical-society-of-australia/article/parkes-observatory-pulsar-data-archive/2784D514AA4258F5048E1DA2F9F9DF57); [2011 archive paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1105.5746)
- **[V]** ASKAP data (CRAFT/VAST/EMU products) are served by CASDA; the endpoints coded into `astroquery.casda` are SIA2 `https://casda.csiro.au/casda_vo_tools/sia2/query`, data access `https://casda.csiro.au/casda_data_access/`, and a login-check URL `https://data.csiro.au/casda_vo_proxy/vo/tap/availability`; the module takes a username, i.e. downloads need an account — [astroquery on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/astroquery/)

#### FAST

### Inferences
- A realistic "catalogue spine" for Stargazer is: psrqpy (pulsars, glitches, GC pulsars) + RRATalog CSV + CHIME/FRB Catalog 2 table + TNS (needs a registered bot) + a hand-maintained LPT table (no catalogue exists). All are megabyte-scale and can be cached locally.
- PTA products are the easiest high-quality time series to work with (tens to hundreds of MB, ASCII, DOI-versioned, readable with PINT), but they are pulse-averaged arrival times, not individual pulses.
- Raw-data archives are open in principle but differ in friction: Zenodo (no account) < CANFAR DOIs and CSIRO DAP (public after embargo; accounts for some functions) < FAST/NADC (registration, large files) < collaboration-internal APIs (not usable).
- Several aggregator sites (FRBSTATS, `cfod`, legacy FRBCAT) look stale; the tool should treat CHIME's own portal, TNS and Blinkverse as the live FRB sources and record a retrieval date and catalogue version with every query (psrqpy supports version pinning).
- For this project's own sandbox, none of the astronomy hosts were reachable; only PyPI was. Hosts that would have to be allow-listed to exercise these sources include `www.atnf.csiro.au`, `www.jb.man.ac.uk`, `psrweb.jb.man.ac.uk`, `zenodo.org`, `data.csiro.au`, `casda.csiro.au`, `www.chime-frb.ca`, `www.canfar.net`, `www.wis-tns.org`, `blinkverse.alkaidos.cn`, `www.herta-experiment.org`, `rratalog.github.io`, `github.com`, `nadc.china-vo.org` and `pulsars.org.au`.

## 2. Periodic and quasi-periodic behaviour in FRBs and unusual transients, including the long-period radio transients found since 2022

### Takeaway
Two repeating FRBs have well-studied periodic *activity windows* (FRB 20180916B, 16.35 d; FRB 20121102A, ~157–160 d, now weakened by FAST non-detections), two more have 2025 claims (FRB 20240209A ~126 d; FRB 20240114A ~143 d, contested), one burst shows a 216.8 ms periodicity inside a single event (FRB 20191221A, 6.5σ), and no repeater has yielded a secure, persistent spin period. Long-period transients have grown from one object in 2022 to roughly 12–15 by late 2026, with periods from 7 minutes to 6.5 hours; a growing subset are now explained as white-dwarf binaries, while the apparently isolated ones (and the emission mechanism in all of them) remain unexplained.

### Cited Findings

#### Periodic activity windows in repeating FRBs
- **[V]** FRB 20180916B: CHIME/FRB found a **16.35 ± 0.15 day** period from 38 bursts (16 Sep 2018 – 4 Feb 2020); all bursts fall in a ~5-day phase window and half within 0.6 days. Later analyses with larger samples give 16.32–16.38 d, within the original error — [CHIME/FRB periodicity paper record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/517779); [arXiv:2310.16307](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.16307v2); [Low-frequency FRB review, arXiv:2203.04890](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.04890)
- **[V]** The activity window is chromatic: simultaneous Apertif and LOFAR observations detected bursts down to 120 MHz and found the window depends on frequency, which "strongly disfavours" models where absorption in a companion's wind produces the periodicity. The search summary's wording on the direction of the effect was truncated and read "lower frequencies"; **[B]** from memory, the published abstract says the window is narrower and earlier at *higher* frequencies (arXiv ID also from memory). Verify before quoting — [Pastor-Marazuela et al. 2021](https://typeset.io/pdf/chromatic-periodic-activity-down-to-120-megahertz-in-a-fast-1rrnfrzqup.pdf); [arXiv:2012.08348](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08348)
- **[V]** FRB 20121102A: Effelsberg L-band monitoring gives a best period of **159.3 ± 0.8 d** (159.3 ± 0.3 d in a normalised-rate model), with S-band activity ending ~19 days before L-band activity — [arXiv:2408.12567](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12567)
- **[V]** But a 2025 FAST "decadal evolution" study finds a best fit of 156.9 d with a ~62% duty cycle and notes that FAST saw no bursts in some predicted active windows; including all non-detections lowers the periodicity significance to S/N 4.3, making the period "less robust" — [arXiv:2507.15790](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15790)
- **[V]** Interpretations in print include orbital motion, precession, and (Katz 2024) a 157-day accretion-disc precession period with a separate 4.605-day candidate orbital period — [arXiv:2405.03721](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.03721)
- **[V]** FRB 20240209A: a 2025 paper reports a possible **~126-day** activity period; a March 2026 A&A paper treats it as the third periodic repeater with an active window covering nearly 70% of the cycle — [arXiv:2502.11215](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11215); [A&A 707, A335 (2026)](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/epub2/2026/03/aa56269-25/aa56269-25.epub)
- **[V]** FRB 20240114A (hyperactive, 11,553 FAST bursts): one team reports a **143.40 ± 7.19 day** periodicity at 5.2σ and three candidate short periodicities near 1.486, 1.576 and 1.865 Hz (3.4–6σ), while the same paper's first version stated no stable periodic signal; an independent periodogram of 3,196 bursts from the most active 4.34-hour window found nothing, and a January 2026 paper reports a failed search for rotation-linked periodicity — [arXiv:2507.14708](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14708v2); [arXiv:2512.24936](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.24936v2)

- **[B]** The large FAST sample of FRB 20121102A (1,652 bursts in 59.5 h over 47 days, peak rate 122 per hour) showed no periodicity between 1 ms and 1,000 s — [arXiv:2107.08205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08205)

#### Sub-second periodicity inside single bursts
- **[V]** FRB 20191221A (CHIME/FRB, *Nature* 607, 256–259, 13 July 2022): ~3 s long with at least nine components separated periodically by **216.8(1) ms, significance 6.5σ**. Two other bursts show weaker hints: FRB 20210206A (2.8(1) ms) and FRB 20210213A (10.7(1) ms). The authors read the periodicity as strong evidence for a neutron-star origin — [arXiv:2107.08463](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08463v2); [Nature paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-04841-8.pdf)
- **[B]** Radio pulsars, magnetars and at least one LPT show quasi-periodic sub-structure whose spacing scales roughly linearly with rotation period (about 10⁻³ of the period); this relation has been proposed as a way to infer rotation periods for FRBs with quasi-periodic components (Kramer et al., *Nature Astronomy* 2024) — [ADS title search](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/search/q=title%3A%22Quasi-periodic%20sub-structure%20in%20the%20emission%20of%20radio-emitting%20neutron%20stars%22)

### Inferences
- "Periodic" covers four physically different things that a tool must keep apart: rotation periods (ms–hours), orbital periods (hours–days), activity-window periods of repeaters (16–160 d, low duty-cycle statistics), and quasi-periodic sub-structure inside one burst. Each needs a different test and a different null hypothesis.
- Activity-window claims are fragile: the best-studied ~157–160 d case lost significance once non-detections were included, and the 2025 claims for FRB 20240114A are internally inconsistent. A tool should always fold exposure/non-detection information into periodicity statistics and report trial-corrected significance.
- Genuinely unexplained as of November 2026: the radio emission mechanism of LPTs; the nature of the apparently isolated LPTs (GLEAM-X J1627, ASKAP J1935, CHIME J0630, ASKAP J1839−0756, ASKAP J1424, CHIME/ILT J1634 with its spin-up); the nature of ASKAP J1832−0911; the physical cause of FRB activity periods; and why no hyperactive repeater shows a stable spin period. "Unexplained" here means "several natural models compete", not "no natural model".
- LPT pulses are minutes long and are found in images, not in pulsar-style searches, so a tool that only ingests high-time-resolution filterbanks would miss this class entirely; an image-domain or light-curve-domain route is needed.

### Gaps
- No single up-to-date, machine-readable LPT list was found; the table above merges a review, Wikipedia and press releases and should be re-checked against the review's tables (the review's own table has at least one typo, 2225 s vs 3225 s for ASKAP J1935+2148).
- The newest official period for FRB 20180916B (post-2023 CHIME updates) was not found, and the direction of the chromatic effect in the search summary conflicted with remembered abstract wording.
- The primary paper for the FRB 20201124A 1.7-s claim and the paper reporting a ~112-day modulation of FRB 20240114A's emission frequency were not individually identified.
- Period derivatives, distances and duty cycles for most LPTs were not collected.

## 3. Methods and software: dedispersion, periodicity and single-pulse searches, classifiers and libraries

### Takeaway
The standard chain is: RFI excision → incoherent dedispersion over many trial DMs → either an FFT search with incoherent harmonic summing (plus acceleration/jerk trials for binaries) or the Fast Folding Algorithm (better for long periods and narrow pulses) → candidate sifting, folding and classification; single pulses are found by matched-filtering dedispersed time series with boxcars and classifying candidates with CNNs such as FETCH. The core tools are open source and mostly alive in 2026 (riptide 0.2.7, sigpyproc 2.0.0 and PINT 1.1.8 were all released in Sept–Oct 2026), but several key ones (PRESTO, Heimdall, TransientX, PulsarX, FETCH, Tempo2) are not on PyPI and their current release state and licences could only partly be confirmed.

### Cited Findings

#### Algorithms
- **[B]** Dispersion: a broadband pulse arrives later at lower frequency by Δt ≈ 4.149 ms × DM × (ν_lo⁻² − ν_hi⁻²) with ν in GHz and DM in pc cm⁻³. *Incoherent* dedispersion shifts filterbank channels by this delay (leaving smearing within each channel); *coherent* dedispersion removes the dispersive phase from the raw voltages and needs baseband data — [Essential Radio Astronomy, pulsar chapter](https://www.cv.nrao.edu/~sransom/web/Ch6.html)
- **[V]** GPU incoherent dedispersion: Barsdell et al. 2012 report a ~9× speed-up of the direct algorithm on a GPU versus an optimised quad-core CPU code (**[B]** this work became the `dedisp` library that Heimdall uses) — [arXiv:1201.5380](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1201.5380)
- **[B]** FFT periodicity search: Fourier-transform each dedispersed time series, sum harmonics incoherently to recover power from narrow pulses, and for binaries search over Fourier-frequency drift ("acceleration") — Ransom, Eikenberry & Middleditch 2002 — [arXiv:astro-ph/0204349](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0204349)
- **[V]** Jerk search: PRESTO's `accelsearch` was extended (from v2.1) to search the frequency second derivative, described by Andersen & Ransom 2018 — [arXiv:1807.07900](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1807.07900)
- **[V]** Fast Folding Algorithm: Morello et al. 2020 show analytically that an FFA search is close to the theoretical optimum and more sensitive than FFT + incoherent harmonic summing across the period range, and that sensitivity estimates need an explicit search-efficiency term; their code found sources that FFT pipelines missed. (A secondary summary quotes an FFA efficiency near 0.93; not checked against the paper) — [arXiv:2004.03701](https://export.arxiv.org/abs/2004.03701); [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020MNRAS.497.4654M)
- **[B]** Single-pulse search: dedisperse, convolve with boxcar filters of increasing width, threshold on S/N, then cluster events across DM, time and width — Cordes & McLaughlin 2003 — [arXiv:astro-ph/0304364](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0304364)
- **[B]** Tree/"fast" dedispersion variants used in real-time FRB systems: FDMT (Zackay & Ofek 2017) and CHIME/FRB's `bonsai` tree dedispersion — [arXiv:1411.5373](https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.5373); [arXiv:1803.11235](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11235)

#### Tools (repository, licence, 2026 status, hardware, thresholds)
- **[V]** **PRESTO** — `github.com/scottransom/presto`; licence GPL-2.0; includes `accelsearch` (acceleration and jerk), `single_pulse_search.py` and `search_bin` (sideband search). The most recent release notes found were for v4.0, a major release that moved to the meson / meson-python build system and removed old Spigot code. A v5 or later 2025–2026 release was not confirmed — [PRESTO on GitHub](https://github.com/scottransom/presto); [repository index](https://science.ecosyste.ms/projects/53694)
- **[B]** PRESTO runs on CPUs (C with Python wrappers); commonly used defaults are a 5σ threshold in `single_pulse_search.py`, harmonic sums of up to 8–32 harmonics and a low initial `accelsearch` sigma cut (≈2) followed by sifting; the usual workflow (rfifind → DDplan → prepsubband → accelsearch → sift → prepfold) is described in Ransom's tutorial — [PRESTO tutorial](https://hosting.astro.cornell.edu/~shami/psrintro/papers/Ransom_PRESTO_tutorial.pdf)
- **[V]** **Heimdall** — GPU single-pulse pipeline (RFI mitigation, incoherent dedispersion, baseline removal, matched filtering, peak finding), distributed at `sourceforge.net/projects/heimdall-astro` (the address given in the `your` README). The 2025 HTRU-North set-up paper chooses Heimdall's S/N threshold to give about one false positive per beam file and uses its DM-tolerance flag to set trial spacing. A December 2025 paper presents "Heimdall++" with better GPU utilisation — [arXiv:1112.0065](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1112.0065); [arXiv:2511.17797](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.17797); [arXiv:2512.00398](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.00398v1); [your on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/your/)
- **[V]** The `your` wrapper `your_heimdall.py` runs Heimdall with defaults DM 10–1000 pc cm⁻³ and a 1.25 S/N-loss tolerance between DM trials on a chosen GPU, illustrating typical settings — [your on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/your/)
- **[V]** **TransientX** — `github.com/ypmen/TransientX`, docs at transientx.readthedocs.io; Men & Barr 2024 (A&A 683, A183). CPU-based (AVX2 + OpenMP), processes data in segments of a few seconds, integrates RFI mitigation, dedispersion, matched filtering, DBSCAN clustering (kd-tree) and candidate plots; about ten times faster than its predecessor BEAR; dedispersion runs in real time on a single CPU core for 4,096 DM trials — [arXiv:2401.13834](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.13834); [TransientX docs](https://transientx.readthedocs.io/en/latest/introduction.html)
- **[V]** **PulsarX** — `github.com/ypmen/PulsarX`; Men et al. 2023 (A&A, DOI 10.1051/0004-6361/202347356). A folding pipeline for large surveys (RFI mitigation, dedispersion, folding of many candidates at once, parameter optimisation, plots) that produces data cubes for visual inspection or machine-learning classifiers; used for the MPIfR–MeerKAT Galactic Plane Survey — [arXiv:2309.02544](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2309.02544); [A&A](https://www.aanda.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202347356)
- **[V]** **FETCH** (Agarwal et al. 2020, MNRAS; ASCL 2005.014): 11 convolutional models taking frequency–time and DM–time images; each has accuracy and recall above 99.5% on a test set of real RFI and pulsar candidates; trained on simulated FRBs plus real RFI from Green Bank telescopes; outputs a probability per candidate. Still used in a 2026 targeted FRB search (GREENBURST pipeline) — [arXiv:1902.06343](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06343v2); [ASCL entry](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020ascl.soft05014A/abstract); [arXiv:2609.04118](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.04118)
- **[V]** FETCH's inputs are made with `your_candmaker.py`, whose defaults produce 256 × 256 (frequency × time) candidate cut-outs and can run on CPU (`-g -1`) or GPUs; the `candies` package (MIT, 2024) exists to generate the same two features (dedispersed dynamic spectrum and DM transform) for the GMRT SPOTLIGHT survey, which expects ~1 PB of data per day — [your on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/your/); [candies on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/candies/)
- **[V]** **riptide** (FFA) — `github.com/v-morello/riptide`; PyPI `riptide-ffa` **0.2.7, released 2026-09-20** (0.2.6 on 2026-09-10 after a gap since Dec 2023); MIT licence; Python ≥3.9; Python interface over C++ kernels; CPU only (example config uses 4 parallel processes, one DM trial each). Provides the `rffa` pipeline and `rseek` quick search — [riptide-ffa on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/riptide-ffa/)
- **[V]** riptide's shipped example configuration is a concrete statement of typical thresholds: three period ranges 0.2–0.5 s, 0.5–2 s and 2–120 s (≈250, 500 and 1000 phase bins); at least 8 periods must fit in the data; peak-finding S/N ≥ 6; final candidates kept at **S/N ≥ 7**; DM trials 0–1000 pc cm⁻³ capped at DM·|sin b| ≤ 45 ("almost no Galactic pulsars are known to have DM × |sin b| > 40"); harmonic flagging and optional removal — [riptide-ffa on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/riptide-ffa/)
- **[V]** **sigpyproc** — `github.com/FRBs/sigpyproc3`; first PyPI release under this name is **2.0.0 (2026-10-17)**, MIT, Python ≥3.12, Linux/macOS, Numba-accelerated; reads SIGPROC filterbank and PSRFITS; README example: `FilReader(...).dedisperse(dm=30.0)` then `series.search(nfolds=4, pmax=10.0)` and `candidates.cluster(snr_min=8.0)`; command-line tools `spp_header`, `spp_view`, `spp_search fft`. The 0.x interface lives on a `legacy` branch (last 0.5.5) — [sigpyproc on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/sigpyproc/)
- **[V]** **your** ("Your Unified Reader") — `github.com/thepetabyteproject/your`; GPL-3.0; reads/writes SIGPROC filterbank and PSRFITS, writes PSRDADA; includes RFI flagging (spectral kurtosis, Savitzky–Golay), candidate making and a Heimdall wrapper; JOSS paper DOI 10.21105/joss.02750. **Last PyPI release 0.6.7 on 2022-01-22**, so it looks unmaintained on PyPI — [your on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/your/)

### Inferences
- Detection thresholds in practice cluster at S/N ≈ 6–8 for both periodicity and single-pulse searches (riptide 6→7, sigpyproc example 8, BLIPSS 7σ, Heimdall tuned to ~1 false positive per beam). With millions of trials, anything below ~7σ is dominated by noise and RFI, so Stargazer should adopt ≥7σ plus an independent confirmation rule.
- For an independent CPU-only tool, the lowest-friction stack is sigpyproc 2.0 (I/O, dedispersion, FFT search) + riptide (FFA, long periods) + PINT (timing), all permissively licensed (MIT/BSD) and released within the two months before this research date. GPL components (`your`, PRESTO, FETCH, Tempo2, `baseband`) can be called as external programs to avoid licence entanglement.
- GPU-only tools (Heimdall) and CNN classifiers (FETCH) matter mainly for raw-data volumes; for catalogue-level and reduced-product analysis they are unnecessary.
- FFA is the right choice for the regime most relevant to "beacon-like" signals (long periods, low duty cycle), exactly why BLIPSS adopted it; FFT + harmonic summing remains better suited to millisecond periods and binaries.

- No head-to-head 2025–2026 benchmark of Heimdall, TransientX, PRESTO and deep-learning searchers was retrieved, and GitHub READMEs could not be read (connector restricted to the project repository).

## 4. Natural phenomenology that can look like "encoding", and its statistical signatures

### Takeaway
Almost every pattern a naive "message detector" would flag (on/off keying, switching between discrete states, regular drifting patterns, rare huge pulses, sudden period jumps, slow wandering of timing, periodic disappearance, frequency-dependent fading) is a documented natural behaviour of pulsars or FRBs with a characteristic statistical fingerprint. The tool must model these as the null hypothesis; the non-random structure seen in real pulsar nulling and the earthquake-like clustering of FRB bursts mean that "not Poisson/not random" is never by itself evidence of artifice.

### Cited Findings
- **[B]** Nulling: pulse emission abruptly switches off for one to many rotations; nulling fractions range from under 1% to over 90%; null and burst lengths are broadly distributed — Wang, Manchester & Johnston 2007; Gajjar, Joshi & Kramer 2012 — [arXiv:astro-ph/0703241](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0703241); [arXiv:1205.2550](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2550)
- **[B]** Nulls are often *not* random: a Wald–Wolfowitz runs test on null/burst sequences found non-randomness (clustering) in most pulsars examined, and some show quasi-periodic nulling — Redman & Rankin 2009 — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.1529R)
- **[B]** Mode changing and state switching: profiles flip between a few discrete stable shapes; in 17 pulsars the spin-down rate switches quasi-periodically between two values, in six cases correlated with pulse-shape changes — Lyne et al. 2010 — [arXiv:1006.5184](https://arxiv.org/abs/1006.5184)
- **[V]** Profile-change "events" also occur in precision-timing millisecond pulsars (three in PSR J1713+0747, one in J1643−1224, a ~10-yr recurrence in B1937+21) — [arXiv:2604.05453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.05453)
- **[B]** Intermittent pulsars: PSR B1931+24 is on for 5–10 days and off for 25–35 days quasi-periodically, with a ~50% higher spin-down rate when on — Kramer et al. 2006 — [arXiv:astro-ph/0604605](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0604605)
- **[B]** RRATs: sporadic single pulses (2–30 ms) separated by minutes to hours, with underlying periods of 0.4–7 s recoverable only from pulse arrival-time differences — McLaughlin et al. 2006 — [arXiv:astro-ph/0511587](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0511587)
- **[B]** Sub-pulse drifting: sub-pulses march across the pulse window with a characteristic spacing (P2) and repetition period (P3), producing diagonal bands in a pulse stack and sharp features in the longitude-resolved and two-dimensional fluctuation spectra; found in at least one-third of 187 pulsars surveyed (likely more than half of all pulsars) — Weltevrede, Edwards & Stappers 2006 — [arXiv:astro-ph/0507282](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0507282)
- **[B]** Pulse-energy statistics: single-pulse energies of 315 pulsars are mostly log-normal (a few Gaussian or exponential), with modulation index varying across the pulse — Burke-Spolaor et al. 2012 — [arXiv:1203.6068](https://arxiv.org/abs/1203.6068)
- **[B]** Giant pulses: the Crab and a few other pulsars emit rare pulses hundreds to thousands of times the mean, with power-law (not log-normal) energy distributions and structure down to nanoseconds (a 0.4-ns, >2 MJy "nanoshot") — Hankins & Eilek 2007; Lundgren et al. 1995 — [arXiv:0708.2505](https://arxiv.org/abs/0708.2505); [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1995ApJ...453..433L)
- **[B]** Glitches: sudden spin-ups with fractional size ~10⁻¹¹–10⁻⁵, often followed by exponential recovery; in most glitching pulsars the waiting times are exponential (Poisson-like) and sizes power-law, while Vela and PSR J0537−6910 are quasi-periodic — Espinoza et al. 2011; Melatos, Peralta & Wyithe 2008 — [arXiv:1102.1743](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.1743); [arXiv:0710.1021](https://arxiv.org/abs/0710.1021)
- **[V]** The live glitch table used by the community is Jodrell Bank's (`https://www.jb.man.ac.uk/pulsar/glitches/gTable.html`), scriptable through psrqpy — [psrqpy on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/psrqpy/)
- **[B]** Timing noise: slow, red-spectrum wandering of arrival times, widespread in young pulsars, sometimes with quasi-periodic structure that can mimic planets or precession — Hobbs, Lyne & Kramer 2010 (366 pulsars) — [arXiv:0912.4537](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.4537)
- **[B]** Interstellar scintillation: diffractive scintillation modulates intensity by ~100% with an exponential intensity distribution over narrow frequency "scintles" and minutes-long timescales (bandwidth scaling steeply, roughly ν⁴); refractive scintillation adds slower 10–30% variations over days to months — Rickett 1990 — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1990ARA%26A..28..561R)
- **[B]** Binary effects: eclipsing "spider" pulsars vanish for ~10% or more of each orbit with frequency-dependent ingress/egress delays (PSR B1957+20); companion winds can also lens pulses, briefly magnifying them by factors of tens near eclipse; orbital motion Doppler-modulates the observed period — Fruchter, Stinebring & Taylor 1988; Main et al. 2018 — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1988Natur.333..237F); [arXiv:1805.09348](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09348)
- **[B]** FRB burst energies (FRB 20121102A, FAST): bimodal distribution with a log-normal bump at a characteristic energy of ~4.8 × 10³⁷ erg plus a high-energy power-law tail — Li et al. 2021 — [arXiv:2107.08205](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.08205)
- **[V]** FRB waiting times: FAST established a bimodal waiting-time distribution (for FRB 20121102A a log-normal peak near ~70 s; **[B]** the second peak is at a few milliseconds); similar bimodality is seen in other repeaters; a pure Poisson process is disfavoured; power-law, exponential and Weibull forms have all been fitted and no single model fits energies or waiting times over the full range — [arXiv:2409.13247](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13247); [arXiv:2306.07585](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.07585)
- **[B]** Weibull fits give a shape parameter well below 1 (k ≈ 0.34 for FRB 121102), i.e. strongly clustered bursts — Oppermann, Yu & Pen 2018 — [arXiv:1705.04881](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.04881)
- **[V]** 2024–2026 work quantifies randomness directly: one study measures the "randomness and scale invariance" of repeater burst sequences; a 2026 preprint on FRB 20240114A finds waiting times that look exponential while the burst rate keeps memory over minutes to about an hour; a September 2026 review summarises the statistical models — [arXiv:2408.02203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.02203); [Emergent Mind summary of arXiv:2608.19713](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2608.19713); [arXiv:2609.25810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25810)
- **[B]** FRB time–energy correlations resemble earthquake aftershock sequences rather than solar flares (Totani & Tsuzuki 2023) — [ADS title search](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/search/q=title%3A%22Fast%20radio%20bursts%20trigger%20aftershocks%20resembling%20earthquakes%22)
- **[B]** At least some FRBs come from magnetars: the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154 emitted an FRB-like burst on 28 April 2020 — [arXiv:2005.10324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.10324); [arXiv:2005.10828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.10828)

### Inferences
- Signature cheat-sheet for the tool: nulling → two-state amplitude sequence with clustered run lengths; mode changing → a small set of discrete profile templates; drifting → narrow peaks in fluctuation spectra at non-integer cycles/period; giant pulses → power-law tail on a log-normal body; glitches → step in spin frequency with recovery; timing noise → red power-law residual spectrum; scintillation → exponential intensity statistics correlated across nearby frequencies and decorrelating over a bandwidth that scales steeply with frequency; eclipses → dropouts locked to orbital phase with excess DM at the edges; FRB repeaters → bimodal waiting times, heavy-tailed energies, aftershock-like clustering.
- Because real nulling fails randomness tests and real FRB sequences show memory, a detector must compare against *physically motivated* surrogates (e.g. Markov two-state nulling, Weibull/aftershock burst processes), not against white noise or a Poisson process.
- Propagation effects are frequency-dependent in known ways (ν⁻² dispersion, steep scaling of scattering and scintillation). An amplitude pattern that is identical across a wide band and survives dedispersion is harder to attribute to propagation, while a pattern confined to scintles is almost certainly propagation.

### Gaps
- Most quantitative statements in this section are from background knowledge and were not re-verified this session (search budget exhausted); exact fractions (e.g. share of pulsars that drift or null in the newest MeerKAT/FAST censuses) were not retrieved.
- No source was retrieved for a standard, published "information content" or entropy analysis of pulsar single-pulse sequences.
- Statistical characterisation of LPT pulse-to-pulse variability (duty cycles, nulling fractions, quasi-periodic substructure) was not collected.

## 5. SETI concepts and actual searches involving pulsars, FRBs or pulsed/periodic radio beacons

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed record contains (a) one historical false alarm resolved in weeks (the first pulsar, "LGM-1", 1967–68), (b) a handful of explicitly speculative proposals (pulsars as beacons, markers or an engineered navigation system; FRBs as light-sail beams), and (c) a small number of real searches for pulsed, artificially dispersed or periodic signals, all of which returned nulls with quantitative limits (Gajjar et al. 2022: 1,883 stars; Suresh et al. 2023 BLIPSS: Galactic Centre). No pulsar, FRB or LPT has credible evidence of artificial origin; claims that pulsars are alien beacons are fringe.

### Cited Findings

#### The first pulsar and why it was judged natural
- **[B]** Hewish, Bell and colleagues reported the first pulsar (period 1.337 s, found at 81.5 MHz by Jocelyn Bell in 1967) in *Nature* in February 1968. The paper notes that man-made origins (space probes, radar, lunar reflections) were ruled out because the source showed no parallax and so lay far outside the Solar System, and that three more similar sources had already been found — [Hewish et al. 1968](https://doi.org/10.1038/217709a0)
- **[B]** The team privately nicknamed the source "LGM-1" (little green men). Hewish's Nobel lecture explains they tested the idea by timing the pulses for weeks to look for the Doppler signature of a transmitter on an orbiting planet; only the Earth's own orbital motion appeared — [Hewish Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1974/hewish/lecture/)
- **[B]** Bell Burnell's own account says the hypothesis collapsed when she found a second pulsing source in a different part of the sky with a different period just before Christmas 1967 (and two more in January): several independent civilisations signalling Earth at the same odd frequency was implausible — [Bell Burnell 1977, "Petit Four"](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1749-6632.1977.tb37085.x)
- **[B]** The natural explanation followed quickly: Gold (1968) proposed rotating magnetised neutron stars, and the discovery of the 33-ms Crab pulsar in a supernova remnant and its measured slow-down confirmed it — [Gold 1968](https://doi.org/10.1038/218731a0)

#### Proposals: pulsars as beacons, markers, clocks or navigation aids (speculative, mostly peer-reviewed)
- **[V]** Vidal, "Pulsar positioning system: a quest for evidence of extraterrestrial engineering" (*International Journal of Astrobiology*, online Nov 2017): notes that millisecond pulsars are clocks comparable to atomic clocks and that X-ray millisecond pulsars provide what a passive galactic positioning system needs, and proposes a "SETI-XNAV" research programme with testable markers (e.g. whether beams favour the Galactic plane, the isotropic distribution of millisecond pulsars). Commentary stresses he does **not** claim pulsars are engineered; it is a research programme, and the paper also criticises the 1967 dismissal of the extraterrestrial hypothesis — [arXiv:1704.03316](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03316v2); [IJA article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-astrobiology/article/pulsar-positioning-system-a-quest-for-evidence-of-extraterrestrial-engineering/E4CCF6D8F46B4B64F7AB99972C30903E); [Centauri Dreams commentary](https://www.centauri-dreams.org/2018/01/15/pulsar-navigation-exploring-an-eti-hypothesis)
- **[B]** Human engineers already use natural pulsars for navigation: NASA's SEXTANT experiment on NICER demonstrated autonomous X-ray pulsar navigation in orbit in 2017–18, which shows usefulness for navigation does not imply design — [NASA release](https://www.nasa.gov/feature/goddard/2018/nasa-team-first-to-demonstrate-x-ray-navigation-in-space)
- **[B]** Edmondson & Stevens (2003), "The utilization of pulsars as SETI beacons": pulsars as natural focal ("Schelling") points, with senders and receivers choosing directions and frequencies tied to bright pulsars; lists candidate star–pulsar alignments — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003IJAsB...2..231E)
- **[B]** Chennamangalam, Siemion, Lorimer & Werthimer (2015), "Jumping the energetics queue": a civilisation could modulate a pulsar's natural beam with an orbiting structure instead of building a transmitter; the signal would look like artificial nulling, so nulling statistics are the place to look — [arXiv:1311.4608](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.4608)
- **[B]** Benford, Benford & Benford (2010): cost-optimised beacons would be short, broadband, narrow-beam pulses revisiting targets rarely, i.e. they would resemble sparse radio transients concentrated toward the Galactic plane/centre — [arXiv:0810.3964](https://arxiv.org/abs/0810.3964); [arXiv:0810.3966](https://arxiv.org/abs/0810.3966)

#### Proposals that FRBs could be artificial
- **[B]** Lingam & Loeb (2017, ApJ Letters), "Fast Radio Bursts from Extragalactic Light Sails": explicitly speculative calculation that FRB energetics and frequencies are consistent with planet-scale beam emitters powering light sails, with bursts seen as the beam sweeps past Earth — [arXiv:1701.01109](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.01109)
- **[B]** Counter-evidence since then: a Galactic magnetar produced an FRB-like burst in 2020, giving a natural engine for at least some FRBs — [arXiv:2005.10324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.10324)
- **[B]** Hippke, Domainko & Learned (2015, arXiv preprint) claimed FRB dispersion measures were integer multiples of 187.5 pc cm⁻³ and floated an artificial origin; the pattern did not survive later detections — [arXiv:1503.05245](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.05245)
- **[B]** Cautionary tale: "perytons", FRB-like swept pulses seen at Parkes, were traced to microwave ovens opened mid-cycle — Petroff et al. 2015 — [arXiv:1504.02165](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.02165)

#### Actual searches (peer-reviewed, null results)
- **[V]** Gajjar et al. 2022 (ApJ 932, 81), "Searching for broadband pulsed beacons from 1883 stars using neural networks": 233 hours of Green Bank Telescope data at 4–8 GHz; searched three classes of signal with **artificial (negative) dispersion**, where low frequencies arrive first, the reverse of interstellar dispersion (an idea credited to Demorest et al. 2004 and Siemion et al. 2010); candidates classified by a custom CNN. **No signal found**; limit: fewer than about 1 in 1,000 nearby stars has a transmitter with power density ≥10⁵ W/Hz repeating at ≤500 s — [arXiv:2205.02964](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02964); [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2022ApJ...932...81G)
- **[V]** Suresh et al. 2023 (AJ; DOI 10.3847/1538-3881/acccf0), BLIPSS: first use of a fast folding algorithm for technosignatures; 4.5 hours of 4–8 GHz Green Bank data on the central 6′ of the Galaxy; searched for kHz-wide signals with periods 11–100 s and duty cycles 10–50%. **Non-detection**; limit: fewer than one in ~600,000 Galactic Centre stars emits such a signal above a 7σ equivalent isotropic radiated power of ≈2 × 10¹⁸ W (10% duty cycle). Rationale: a rotating beacon at the Galactic Centre would be an efficient galaxy-wide broadcaster — [arXiv:2305.18527](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2305.18527); [summary](https://astrobiology.com/2023/05/31/a-4-8-ghz-galactic-center-search-for-periodic-technosignatures/)
- **[B]** Earlier pulse searches with a SETI motivation: Astropulse (SETI@home's microsecond dispersed-pulse search of Arecibo data, which also searched negative DMs) and the Allen Telescope Array "Fly's Eye" survey — von Korff et al. 2013; Siemion et al. 2010 — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013ApJ...767...40V); [arXiv:0811.3046](https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.3046)
- **[B]** Breakthrough Listen's Galactic Centre survey (Gajjar et al. 2021) searched Parkes and Green Bank data for both narrowband and pulsed signals and reported no technosignatures — [arXiv:2104.14148](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14148)
- **[B]** Breakthrough Listen instruments have also produced mainstream FRB science (e.g. 4–8 GHz bursts from FRB 121102), which is natural-source astrophysics, not a technosignature claim — [arXiv:1804.04101](https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04101)

#### Peer-reviewed versus fringe
- **[V]/[B]** Peer-reviewed searches with limits: Gajjar et al. 2022; Suresh et al. 2023 (both [V]); von Korff et al. 2013; Gajjar et al. 2021 ([B]). Peer-reviewed but speculative proposals: Vidal 2019; Edmondson & Stevens 2003; Chennamangalam et al. 2015; Benford et al. 2010; Lingam & Loeb 2017. Preprint-only claim later undermined by data: Hippke et al. 2015 — [arXiv:2205.02964](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02964); [arXiv:2305.18527](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2305.18527)

### Inferences
- The LGM-1 episode supplies a ready-made checklist that still applies: (1) is the signal fixed in sidereal coordinates; (2) is it dispersed like something that crossed the interstellar medium; (3) after correcting for Earth's motion, is there any Doppler signature of a transmitter on an orbiting body; (4) do similar sources appear elsewhere in the sky (a population implies a natural class).
- The two concrete, published discriminators for artificial pulses are *negative or otherwise non-ν⁻² dispersion* (Gajjar et al. 2022) and *narrow-band periodicity* (BLIPSS). Both are cheap to add to a pipeline that already dedisperses and folds, and both have natural false-positive channels (RFI) that explain why the published searches needed CNN vetting and on/off-source logic.
- Proposals such as "modulated pulsars" make a testable prediction that overlaps natural nulling; any search along these lines must show an excess over the natural non-randomness documented in section 4, which is a high bar.
- Fringe claims in circulation (e.g. books asserting that pulsars are alien beacons) have no peer-reviewed astrophysical support; the tool's documentation should cite the null-result searches rather than these.

### Gaps
- The one search run on Vidal's proposal returned no formal published rebuttal or named critique; absence in a single search is not proof that none exists.
- The search budget ran out before criticisms of Lingam & Loeb, Vidal and the pulsar-beacon proposals could be retrieved from primary sources; the criticisms above are background knowledge, and no named rebuttal paper was verified.
- Not retrieved: Vidal's IAU proceedings paper on millisecond pulsars as standards; Sullivan's and Heidmann's early pulsar-synchronised SETI ideas; Zhang's 2020 quantitative assessment of FRB-like artificial signals; exact parameter ranges of Astropulse and of the 2021 Galactic Centre survey; any 2024–2026 follow-ups to BLIPSS (e.g. MeerKAT or FAST periodic-signal searches).
- No source was located this session for specific fringe publications (e.g. LaViolette), so none is cited.
- Details of the three negative-DM signal classes and CNN performance in Gajjar et al. 2022 were not available in the search summary.

## 6. What an independent tool could realistically run on public data, and what data volume and compute that takes

### Takeaway
Three tiers are realistic. Tier 1 (megabytes, a laptop): catalogue- and burst-list statistics on psrcat, RRATalog, CHIME/FRB Catalog 2 and published repeater burst tables. Tier 2 (hundreds of MB to tens of GB, a workstation): timing-residual and single-pulse/dynamic-spectrum analyses on PTA releases and released burst cut-outs. Tier 3 (terabytes to petabytes, a cluster or GPUs): re-searching raw Parkes/FAST filterbanks, which is only sensible for hand-picked pointings. The scientifically defensible outputs are anomaly rankings against natural-emission null models, not detections.

### Cited Findings
- **[V]** Tier 1 inputs are small and current: psrcat 4,393 pulsars (one tarball); RRATalog 335 rows (CSV); CHIME/FRB Catalog 2 4,539 bursts with machine-readable table; FAST burst samples of up to 11,553 bursts for a single repeater (FRB 20240114A) — [ATNF history](https://www.atnf.csiro.au/research/pulsar/psrcat/catalogueHistory.html); [RRATalog](https://rratalog.github.io/rratalog); [arXiv:2601.09399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09399); [arXiv:2507.14708](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14708v2)
- **[V]** Tier 2 inputs: NANOGrav 15-yr (~0.6 GB, 68 pulsars), EPTA DR2 (~39 MB zip, 25 pulsars), MeerKAT PTA (245,907 TOAs, 83 pulsars), all readable with PINT (BSD) — [Zenodo 8423265](https://zenodo.org/records/8423265); [Zenodo 8300645](https://zenodo.org/records/8300645); [arXiv:2412.01148](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.01148); [pint-pulsar on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pint-pulsar/)
- **[V]** Searching PTA timing data for periodicities is established practice: a 2024 paper ran a periodicity search on the timing of all 25 EPTA DR2 pulsars — [arXiv:2408.09795](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.09795)
- **[V]** Tier 2 burst-level products: CHIME/FRB Catalog 2 dynamic spectra at CANFAR (doi:10.11570/25.0066); 140 beamformed baseband bursts in HDF5; thousands of FAST dynamic spectra via Blinkverse — [arXiv:2601.09399](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.09399); [arXiv:2311.00111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.00111); [Blinkverse paper](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-1997/9/7/330)
- **[V]** Tier 3 scale: the Parkes archive is >4.5 PB in nearly 2 million files (public after 18 months); modern surveys can produce ~1 PB per day (GMRT SPOTLIGHT estimate) — [arXiv:2511.22702](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.22702); [candies on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/candies/)
- **[V]** Compute anchors: BLIPSS ran its periodic-signal FFA search on 4.5 hours of 4–8 GHz Green Bank data on CPUs; TransientX dedisperses 4,096 DM trials in real time on one CPU core; riptide's example pipeline is configured for 4 CPU processes; a GPU gives roughly 9× over a quad-core CPU for brute-force dedispersion — [arXiv:2305.18527](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2305.18527); [arXiv:2401.13834](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.13834); [riptide-ffa on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/riptide-ffa/); [arXiv:1201.5380](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1201.5380)
- **[V]** Third parties do find new things in public archives: 19 pulsars were found in FAST archival releases by an independent search (Jan 2026), and riptide's README shows a faint pulsar recovered blind from survey data — [arXiv:2601.01912](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.01912.md); [riptide-ffa on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/riptide-ffa/)
- **[V]** Generic, maintained time-series building blocks on PyPI: `stingray` (v2.3.2, 2026-03-13, MIT; timing methods developed for X-ray data), `nifty-ls` (fast Lomb–Scargle, v1.1.0, 2025-08-02), `p4j` (information-theoretic periodograms, v1.1.2, 2024-05-24) — [stingray](https://pypi.org/project/stingray/); [nifty-ls](https://pypi.org/project/nifty-ls/); [p4j](https://pypi.org/project/p4j/)
- **[V]** Published precedent for statistics the tool could replicate on burst lists: Weibull/log-normal/power-law fits to waiting times and energies, tests of Poisson behaviour, and "randomness and scale-invariance" measures for repeaters — [arXiv:2306.07585](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.07585); [arXiv:2408.02203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.02203)
- **[B]** Published precedent for single-pulse-sequence tests: runs tests on null/burst sequences, and fluctuation-spectrum analysis for drifting — [ADS: Redman & Rankin 2009](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2009MNRAS.395.1529R); [arXiv:astro-ph/0507282](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0507282)

### Inferences
- **Analyses that are feasible and meaningful (Tier 1–2):**
  1. *Pulse-energy distribution tests* per source: fit log-normal vs. power-law vs. mixtures; flag sources whose distribution is discrete/quantised or has too few distinct levels (natural emission is continuous and heavy-tailed).
  2. *Modulation index and fluctuation spectra* (longitude-resolved and two-dimensional): natural drifting gives one or two broad features; flag multiple exact, phase-stable, harmonically unrelated modulation frequencies.
  3. *Two-state sequence tests* on nulling data: run-length distributions, runs tests, Markov-order estimation, block entropy and compression ratio, each compared with Markov-chain surrogates fitted to the same source rather than with coin-flips.
  4. *Waiting-time tests* for repeaters and RRATs: exponential vs. Weibull vs. log-normal mixture; check for excess at exact multiples of a base interval after barycentring; always fold in exposure windows (e.g. CHIME's once-per-sidereal-day sampling creates aliases).
  5. *Timing tests* with PINT on PTA or single-source TOAs: residual periodograms, glitch/step detection, and a "too stable" check (period derivative consistent with zero or negative without a binary/accretion explanation; note the natural counter-example CHIME/ILT J1634+44).
  6. *Dispersion-law tests* on dynamic spectra: fit the delay exponent (expect −2), test for negative DM, and compare DM with NE2001/YMW16/NE2025 expectations for that sightline using `pygedm`/`mwprop`.
  7. *Bandwidth/periodicity test à la BLIPSS*: per-channel FFA for narrow-band periodic power.
  8. *Catalogue cross-match* of every candidate against psrcat, RRATalog, CHIME repeaters and the LPT table before any further analysis.
- **Rough scale estimates (my arithmetic, not sourced):** the Parkes archive averages ~2.3 GB per file (4,624 TB / 2 million). A 35-minute, 96-channel, 1-bit, 250-μs legacy survey beam is ~100 MB; a 9-minute, 1024-channel, 2-bit, 64-μs beam is ~2 GB. A sequence of 10⁴–10⁵ single pulses with a few hundred phase bins is tens of MB once dedispersed, so pulse-sequence statistics for one source are laptop work once someone has reduced the raw data; the cost sits in dedispersion and RFI cleaning, not in the statistics.
- A CPU-only workstation can run FFA and single-pulse searches on individual pointings (minutes to hours each); systematic re-processing of thousands of pointings needs a cluster or GPUs and substantial egress from the archive, so Stargazer should default to Tiers 1–2 and treat Tier 3 as an opt-in "targeted pointing" mode.
- Public single-pulse archives are the bottleneck: most open products are pulse-averaged (profiles, TOAs) or per-burst cut-outs. Full single-pulse time series for nulling/drifting pulsars are mostly obtainable only by re-reducing raw search-mode or fold-mode files.
- Any reported "anomaly" should carry: trial-factor-corrected significance, the natural null models it was tested against, the catalogue versions used, and an RFI checklist (zero-DM power, topocentric vs. barycentric period stability, multi-beam/multi-site coincidence).

### Gaps
- No published end-to-end compute benchmark (CPU-hours per GB or per pointing) for PRESTO/riptide/TransientX on archival data was retrieved; the scale figures above are derived, not cited.
- The size of the CHIME/FRB Catalog 2 dynamic-spectra bundle and of the baseband release was not confirmed.
- A survey of which public archives hold ready-made single-pulse time series (e.g. MeerKAT Thousand-Pulsar-Array single-pulse data, FAST burst cut-outs on ScienceDB, LOFAR long-term archive) could not be done before the search budget was exhausted.
- General technosignature statistics (information-theoretic tests, false-alarm control) are covered by another researcher and were deliberately not expanded here.
